<Article id="why" title="Why this matters for your self-model">

Linearize the compliant pendulum around $\theta_a = 0$ and you get $I\ddot\theta_a = -(k + mgL)\,\theta_a + k\,\theta_m - c\,\dot\theta_a$. Take away the motor and the damping and that's Feynman's harmonic oscillator, $m\ddot x = -kx$, with an effective stiffness $k + mgL$. Everything you care about downstream, including natural frequency, resonance, the mass bump's detuning, and the AUC = 0.11 result, is built on this one equation. Feynman explains *why* the solution is a sinusoid and why its frequency doesn't depend on amplitude. The build makes you face a practical question too: your simulator is the ground truth for your self-model's training data, so does it conserve energy, or is it quietly pumping some in?

</Article>

<Article id="ideas" title="Key ideas: harmonic motion">

- **Restoring force proportional to displacement gives sinusoids.** $\ddot x = -\omega_0^2 x$ is solved by $x = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$, where $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ (or $\sqrt{(k + mgL)/I}$ for your arm).
- **Frequency doesn't depend on amplitude, as long as the system is linear.** Small and big swings take the same time. The real pendulum breaks this at large angles because $\sin\theta \neq \theta$.
- **Harmonic motion is the shadow of circular motion.** A point going around a circle at angular speed $\omega_0$, projected onto a line. That picture becomes complex numbers next lesson.
- **Initial conditions set amplitude and phase; the system sets frequency.** Two numbers ($x_0, v_0$) in, two numbers ($A, \phi$) out.
- **Energy sloshes between kinetic and potential and the total is constant.** $E = \tfrac12 v^2 + \tfrac12\omega_0^2 x^2$ (per unit mass). That makes it a perfect check on a simulator.
- **Linearity means you can add solutions.** Any two solutions sum to a solution. That's why a drive can be analysed one frequency at a time.
- **Integrators differ in what they get wrong.** Explicit Euler gains energy every step; semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler keeps it bounded; RK4 is very accurate per step but still drifts slightly over very long runs.

</Article>

<Q id="p1" title="Frequency and period" answer="4.4508;0.7084;1.4117" labels="ω0 (rad/s);f (Hz);T (s)" tol="0.002">

The linearized arm has $I = 1$ kg·m² and effective stiffness $\kappa = k + mgL = 19.81$ N·m/rad. Find $\omega_0$, the frequency in Hz, and the period.

*Enter $\omega_0$, $f$ and $T$.*

<Solution>

$\omega_0 = \sqrt{19.81} = 4.4508$ rad/s, $f = \omega_0/2\pi = 0.7084$ Hz, $T = 1/f = 1.4117$ s.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p2" title="Initial conditions → amplitude and phase" answer="0.1504;-0.8434" labels="A (rad);φ (rad)" tol="0.002">

With $\omega_0 = 4.4508$ rad/s, the arm starts at $\theta(0) = 0.1$ rad with $\dot\theta(0) = 0.5$ rad/s. Write $\theta(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$ and find $A$ and $\phi$.

*Enter $A$ and $\phi$.*

<Solution>

$\theta(0) = A\cos\phi = 0.1$ and $\dot\theta(0) = -A\omega_0\sin\phi = 0.5$, so $A\sin\phi = -0.5/4.4508 = -0.11234$.
$A = \sqrt{0.1^2 + 0.11234^2} = 0.15040$ rad, $\phi = \operatorname{atan2}(-0.11234, 0.1) = -0.8434$ rad.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p3" title="Energy as a cross-check" answer="0.22405" labels="E" tol="0.001">

Compute the energy per unit inertia $E = \tfrac12\dot\theta^2 + \tfrac12\omega_0^2\theta^2$ for Problem 2's initial state. Use it to recover the amplitude without trigonometry.

*Enter $E$.*

<Solution>

$E = \tfrac12(0.25) + \tfrac12(19.81)(0.01) = 0.125 + 0.09905 = 0.22405$.
At maximum swing $\dot\theta = 0$, so $\tfrac12\omega_0^2A^2 = E \Rightarrow A = \sqrt{2E}/\omega_0 = \sqrt{0.4481}/4.4508 = 0.15040$ rad ✓.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p4" title="Feynman's forced oscillator, for your pendulum" answer="0.2775;-0.1853" labels="coefficient at ω = 3;coefficient at ω = 6" tol="0.005">

Ignoring damping, the motor $\theta_m = A_d\cos\omega t$ drives $I\ddot\theta = -\kappa\theta + kA_d\cos\omega t$. Following 21-5, the steady state is $\theta = \frac{(k/I)A_d}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\cos\omega t$. Evaluate the amplitude with $A_d = 0.3$, $k = 10$, $I = 1$, at $\omega = 3$ and at $\omega = 6$ rad/s. What happens to the sign?

*Enter the signed coefficient $\frac{(k/I)A_d}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}$ at each frequency (negative = antiphase).*

<Solution>

$\omega = 3$: $3/(19.81 - 9) = 0.2775$ rad, in phase with the motor.
$\omega = 6$: $3/(19.81 - 36) = -0.1853$, so amplitude 0.185 rad, **in antiphase**: the arm swings opposite to the motor.
The sign flips as $\omega$ passes $\omega_0$. Damping turns this flip into a smooth 180° phase sweep (the resonance lesson).

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p5" title="Is amplitude independent of frequency?" choice="c">

Feynman says the free oscillator's frequency doesn't depend on amplitude. Your real pendulum, swinging to 1 rad, takes noticeably longer per cycle than at 0.1 rad. Which assumption broke? By roughly how much is the gravity-only pendulum's period longer at 1 rad?

**Which assumption broke?**

<Choice v="a">Energy conservation</Choice>
<Choice v="b">The damping model</Choice>
<Choice v="c">Linearity: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ fails, so the restoring torque grows less than linearly</Choice>
<Choice v="d">The spring constant changes with angle</Choice>

<Solution>

Linearity: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ fails at large angles. The restoring torque grows *less* than linearly, so the pendulum is "softer" at big swings and slower. For a gravity-only pendulum, $T \approx T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16 + \dots)$, so at $\theta_0 = 1$ rad the period is about 6–7% longer. In a self-model, amplitude-dependent frequency is a classic source of extrapolation error when training data only contains small swings.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p6" title="How explicit Euler gains energy" answer="7.2" labels="energy factor after 10 s" tol="0.03">

For $\ddot x = -\omega_0^2 x$, explicit Euler is $x' = x + h v$, $v' = v - h\omega_0^2 x$. Show that $\omega_0^2 x'^2 + v'^2 = (1 + h^2\omega_0^2)(\omega_0^2 x^2 + v^2)$. With $h = 0.01$ and $\omega_0^2 = 19.81$, how much has the energy grown after 10 s?

*Enter the energy growth factor after 10 s.*

<Solution>

$\omega_0^2(x + hv)^2 + (v - h\omega_0^2x)^2 = \omega_0^2x^2 + 2h\omega_0^2xv + h^2\omega_0^2v^2 + v^2 - 2h\omega_0^2xv + h^2\omega_0^4x^2$
$= (1 + h^2\omega_0^2)(\omega_0^2x^2 + v^2)$. The cross terms cancel.
Per step: $\times 1.001981$. After 1000 steps: $1.001981^{1000} \approx e^{1.98} \approx 7.2\times$ the energy. The build tests this exact factor.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p7" title="Which simulator would you train a self-model on?" choice="a">

You're generating training data for a learned forward model. Explicit Euler at $h = 0.01$, semi-implicit Euler at $h = 0.01$, or RK4 at $h = 0.01$? What goes wrong with the wrong choice?

**Which one should you NOT train a self-model on?**

<Choice v="a">Explicit Euler</Choice>
<Choice v="b">Semi-implicit Euler</Choice>
<Choice v="c">RK4</Choice>
<Choice v="d">All three are equally fine</Choice>

<Solution>

Not explicit Euler: its "physics" includes free energy, so the learned model inherits a fictitious negative damping and will be confidently wrong about how fast oscillations decay. That's exactly the quantity a resonance-based detector depends on. Semi-implicit Euler gets the energy behaviour right (bounded, no drift) and is cheap; it's the standard in robotics simulators. RK4 is the most accurate per step and fine at this $h$. Whatever you pick, **validate the simulator against an invariant** (energy, period) before trusting any model trained on its output.

</Solution>
