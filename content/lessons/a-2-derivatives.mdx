<Article id="why" title="Why this matters for your self-model">

Every gradient-based self-model, from a fitted physical parameter to a Dreamer world model, runs on one quantity: $\partial(\text{prediction})/\partial(\text{parameter})$. How much would the predicted arm angle change if the mass were a little different? If you can compute that, you can fit the mass from data (Gauss–Newton or gradient descent), estimate how detectable a mass change is at a given drive frequency, and know which parameters your data actually pins down. 18.01's derivative rules (product, quotient, chain, implicit) are exactly the rules an automatic-differentiation engine applies mechanically. In this build you write that engine yourself, about 60 lines of dual numbers, and use it to recover the arm's mass from four seconds of encoder data.

</Article>

<Article id="ideas" title="Key ideas: derivatives and sensitivities">

- **The derivative is the best linear approximation.** $f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h$. Everything else is rules for computing $f'$.
- **Three rules cover almost everything:** product $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$, quotient, and chain $(f\circ g)' = f'(g)\,g'$. Together with the derivatives of $\sin$, $\cos$, $e^x$, $\ln x$ and $x^n$.
- **Sensitivity is a derivative with respect to a parameter, not time.** $\partial\omega_n/\partial m$, $\partial\ddot\theta/\partial k$, $\partial\theta(t)/\partial m$. The same rules apply.
- **Implicit differentiation handles constraints.** The bob on a circle, $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$, gives $dy/dx = -x/y$ without solving for $y$.
- **Limits are where small-angle physics comes from.** $\lim_{h\to0}\sin h/h = 1$ is the statement that $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, the linearization behind every lesson so far.
- **Forward-mode autodiff = carry $(v, \dot v)$ through the program.** Each operation applies its derivative rule. The result is exact, unlike a finite difference, which has to pick a step size.
- **Sensitivities propagate through time.** $\partial\theta_{t+1}/\partial m$ depends on $\partial\theta_t/\partial m$. Differentiating a rollout is the chain rule applied once per time step: backpropagation through time, run forwards.
- **Gauss–Newton uses sensitivities to fit.** Residuals times sensitivities, divided by sensitivities squared, gives the step. It converges in a handful of iterations for well-excited data.

</Article>

<Q id="p1" title="Product rule" answer="2.9574" labels="f'(1.3)" tol="0.001">

Differentiate $f(x) = x^2\sin x$. Evaluate at $x = 1.3$.

*Enter $f'(1.3)$.*

<Solution>

$f'(x) = 2x\sin x + x^2\cos x$. At 1.3: $2.6\cdot0.96356 + 1.69\cdot0.26750 = 2.5053 + 0.4521 = 2.9574$.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p2" title="Stiffness sensitivity of the resonance" answer="0.11234" labels="∂ωn/∂k" tol="0.002">

$\omega_n = \sqrt{(k + mgL)/(mL^2)}$. Compute $\partial\omega_n/\partial k$ at the defaults. If you swapped in a spring 10% stiffer, roughly how far would the resonance move?

*Enter $\partial\omega_n/\partial k$.*

<Solution>

Chain rule: $\partial\omega_n/\partial k = \dfrac{1}{2\omega_n}\cdot\dfrac{1}{mL^2} = \dfrac{1}{2\cdot4.4508} = 0.11234$ rad/s per N·m/rad.
$\Delta k = 1 \Rightarrow \Delta\omega_n \approx 0.112$ rad/s (exact: $\sqrt{20.81} - \sqrt{19.81} = 0.1111$).

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p3" title="Mass sensitivity, and where linearization fails" answer="-1.1234;3.889" labels="∂ωn/∂m;linear prediction of ωn" tol="0.003">

Compute $\partial\omega_n/\partial m$ at $m = 1$. Use it to predict $\omega_n$ after the ×1.5 bump, and compare to the exact 4.059 rad/s.

*Enter the derivative and the linear prediction of $\omega_n$ after the bump.*

<Solution>

$\omega_n^2 = k/(mL^2) + g/L$, so $2\omega_n\,\partial\omega_n/\partial m = -k/(m^2L^2)$, giving $\partial\omega_n/\partial m = -10/(2\cdot4.4508) = -1.1234$ rad/s per kg.
Linear prediction for $\Delta m = 0.5$: $4.451 - 0.562 = 3.889$ rad/s, versus exact 4.059. The derivative overshoots because $\omega_n(m)$ curves (its slope flattens as $m$ grows). A 50% change is not "small", so for big body changes, re-solve rather than linearize.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p4" title="Implicit differentiation: the bob's path" answer="-0.577" labels="dy/dx at 30°" tol="0.005">

The bob moves on $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$ ($y$ measured downward from the pivot). Find $dy/dx$, and evaluate it at $\theta = 30°$ ($x = L\sin\theta$, $y = L\cos\theta$). What does it mean physically?

*Enter $dy/dx$ at 30°.*

<Solution>

$2x + 2y\,y' = 0 \Rightarrow dy/dx = -x/y = -\tan\theta$. At 30°: $-0.577$.
It's the slope of the bob's path: the velocity is tangent to the circle, at angle $\theta$ from horizontal. No need to solve $y = \sqrt{L^2 - x^2}$ first.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p5" title="Inverse function: angle from a camera" answer="0.002;0.001" labels="error at x = 0 (rad);error at x = 1 m (rad)">

A camera tracks the bob and gives you $x$ and $y$ in meters. You compute $\theta = \arctan(x/y)$. If $y = 1$ m and the camera's $x$ estimate has 2 mm of error, how much angle error does that cause at $x = 0$? At $x = 1$ m?

*Enter both angle errors.*

<Solution>

$\dfrac{d}{du}\arctan u = \dfrac{1}{1+u^2}$, with $u = x/y$ and $du/dx = 1/y = 1$.
At $x = 0$: $d\theta/dx = 1$, so 2 mm gives 0.002 rad. At $x = 1$: $d\theta/dx = 1/2$, so 0.001 rad.
Sensitivity depends on where you are, which is why error bars on derived quantities need derivatives.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p6" title="Sensitivity through one time step" choice="c">

One semi-implicit step: $\omega' = \omega + \Delta t\,\alpha(\theta, \omega; k)$, $\theta' = \theta + \Delta t\,\omega'$, with $\alpha = [k(\theta_m - \theta) - mgL\sin\theta - c\omega]/I$. Starting from a state that doesn't depend on $k$, compute $\partial\omega'/\partial k$ and $\partial\theta'/\partial k$. What changes on the *second* step?

**Which is right after one step?**

<Choice v="a">$\partial\theta'/\partial k = (\theta_m - \theta)/I$</Choice>
<Choice v="b">$\partial\theta'/\partial k = 0$ after one step</Choice>
<Choice v="c">$\partial\theta'/\partial k = \Delta t^2(\theta_m - \theta)/I$</Choice>
<Choice v="d">$\partial\theta'/\partial k = \Delta t(\theta_m - \theta)$</Choice>

<Solution>

$\partial\alpha/\partial k = (\theta_m - \theta)/I$, so $\partial\omega'/\partial k = \Delta t(\theta_m - \theta)/I$ and $\partial\theta'/\partial k = \Delta t\,\partial\omega'/\partial k = \Delta t^2(\theta_m - \theta)/I$.
On the second step, $\theta$ and $\omega$ themselves depend on $k$, so the chain rule picks up extra terms like $\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial k}$. Sensitivities accumulate step by step. Dual numbers do this bookkeeping for you.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p7" title="How good is sin θ ≈ θ?" answer="1.516;18.84" labels="error at 0.3 rad (%);error at 1 rad (%)" tol="0.02">

Use $\lim_{h\to0}\sin h/h = 1$ and the next term of the expansion, $\sin\theta \approx \theta - \theta^3/6$, to estimate the relative error of the small-angle approximation at $\theta = 0.3$ rad and $\theta = 1$ rad.

*Enter the exact relative error $(\theta - \sin\theta)/\sin\theta$ in percent at each angle.*

<Solution>

Relative error $\approx \theta^2/6$: 1.5% at 0.3 rad (exact: $0.3$ vs $0.29552$, so 1.5% ✓) and about 17% at 1 rad (exact $0.8415$, 18.8%).
The linear models in Month 1–2 are good to ~1% for swings under 0.3 rad and unreliable past ~0.7 rad.

</Solution>

<Q id="p8" title="Why exact derivatives, not finite differences?" choice="b">

You could estimate $\partial\theta(T)/\partial m$ by re-running the rollout with $m + h$. Give two reasons the dual-number version is better, and one situation where finite differences are still useful.

**Which summary is right?**

<Choice v="a">Dual numbers are faster than a single rollout</Choice>
<Choice v="b">They are exact (no step-size trade-off) and need no tuning; finite differences are still the standard way to test them</Choice>
<Choice v="c">Finite differences can't differentiate through sin</Choice>
<Choice v="d">There is no real difference</Choice>

<Solution>

(1) **Accuracy:** a finite difference trades truncation error (big $h$) against floating-point cancellation (tiny $h$); dual numbers are exact to machine precision. (2) **No step size to tune**, so it's robust across parameters with very different scales. (Forward mode costs about one extra rollout per parameter; reverse mode, i.e. backprop, gets all parameters at once.)
Finite differences remain the standard way to *test* an autodiff implementation, which is exactly what one of the build tests does.

</Solution>
</Q>
