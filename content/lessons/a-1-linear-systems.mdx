<Article id="why" title="Why this matters for your self-model">

A forward model maps causes to effects: (state, motor command) → next state. Self-modeling constantly asks the reverse question. *Given what I just observed, what command or what body must have produced it?* That's solving $Ax = b$. Whether that question has a unique answer is decided by the determinant. If two different bodies, say mass $m$ and mass $1.5m$, produce the *same* observations under your excitation, the relevant matrix is singular, and no detector, however clever, can tell them apart. Some low-AUC results come from exactly this: a detection problem that was never identifiable under the data collected. This lesson gives you the tools to check that before you train anything.

</Article>

<Article id="ideas" title="Key ideas: Ax = b and the determinant">

- **$Ax = b$ asks "which input lands on $b$?"** Solving a linear system means inverting the transformation for one specific output.
- **The determinant is the volume scale factor.** $|\det A| \gt 1$ stretches, $|\det A| \lt 1$ shrinks, and $\det A = 0$ flattens space, so some inputs become indistinguishable.
- **Null space = what you can't see.** If $Av = 0$ for some $v \neq 0$, then $x$ and $x + v$ give identical outputs. In self-modeling terms, a change in the null-space direction is invisible.
- **Triangular systems are easy.** Lower-triangular: solve top to bottom (forward substitution). Upper-triangular: bottom to top (back substitution).
- **LU factorization is Gaussian elimination with a memory.** $A = LU$ (or $PA = LU$ with row swaps). Factor once, then each new right-hand side costs only two substitutions.
- **Pivot on the biggest entry.** Dividing by a tiny pivot amplifies rounding error catastrophically. Partial pivoting (swap the largest remaining entry into place) fixes it.
- **Don't compute $A^{-1}$; solve.** It's slower and less accurate. "Apply the inverse" in code means "call a solver".
- **Inverse dynamics is a solve.** Given measured $\theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta$, recover the motor command or torque that produced them. This is the backbone of many self-model consistency checks.

</Article>

<Q id="p1" title="What the Euler step does to area" answer="1.000981" labels="det A_d" tol="0.000005">

From lesson 1, $A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.01 \\ -0.1981 & 0.999\end{bmatrix}$. Compute $\det A_d$. What does it say about how explicit Euler treats the pendulum's energy over many steps?

*Enter $\det A_d$ (all the digits).*

<Solution>

$\det A_d = 1\cdot 0.999 - 0.01\cdot(-0.1981) = 0.999 + 0.001981 = 1.000981$.

Each step scales the area of any patch of $(\theta, \dot\theta)$ space by about $1.001$. The real damped pendulum should *shrink* areas (damping removes energy), so explicit Euler is injecting energy, about 0.1% in area per step. Over 1000 steps that's $1.000981^{1000} \approx 2.67\times$. This is the drift you saw in the lesson-1 plot; lesson 4 explains it with eigenvalues.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p2" title="Forward substitution" answer="2;1;1.5" labels="y₁;y₂;y₃">

Solve $Ly = b$ with $L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, $b = [4, 5, 6]^\top$.

*Enter $y$.*

<Solution>

$y_1 = 4/2 = 2$. $y_2 = (5 - 1\cdot 2)/3 = 1$. $y_3 = (6 - (-1)(2) - 2\cdot 1)/4 = 6/4 = 1.5$.
Check row 3: $-2 + 2 + 6 = 6$. ✓

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p3" title="LU by hand" answer="1;1" labels="x₁;x₂">

Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ as $LU$ (no pivoting), then solve $Ax = [3, 9]^\top$ using two substitutions.

*Enter $x$.*

<Solution>

Multiplier $\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$. Subtract $2\times$ row 1 from row 2: $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}$, $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
$Ly = b$: $y = [3,\; 9 - 6] = [3, 3]$. $Ux = y$: $x_2 = 1$, $x_1 = (3 - 1)/2 = 1$. So $x = [1, 1]$.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p4" title="A singular system" choice="a">

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Find $\det A$, the column space, and the null space. How many solutions does $Ax = [1, 2]^\top$ have? What about $Ax = [1, 0]^\top$?

**How many solutions in each case?**

<Choice v="a">$b = [1,2]$: infinitely many solutions. $b = [1,0]$: none.</Choice>
<Choice v="b">$b = [1,2]$: exactly one. $b = [1,0]$: none.</Choice>
<Choice v="c">$b = [1,2]$: none. $b = [1,0]$: infinitely many.</Choice>
<Choice v="d">Both have exactly one solution.</Choice>

<Solution>

$\det A = 4 - 4 = 0$. Both columns are multiples of $[1, 2]$, so the column space is the line spanned by $[1,2]$. Null space: $x_1 + 2x_2 = 0$, spanned by $[2, -1]$.
$b = [1,2]$ is on the line, so there are infinitely many solutions: $x = [1, 0] + t[2,-1]$. $b = [1, 0]$ is off the line, so there are none.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p5" title="Identifiability: when mass and gravity look the same" choice="b">

You log the pendulum hanging freely with the motor held at $\theta_m = 0$ and only small swings. You try to estimate $p_1 = k/I$ and $p_2 = g/L$ separately from $\ddot\theta = -p_1\theta - p_2\sin\theta - (c/I)\dot\theta$. Why does this fail? Name two ways to fix the experiment.

**Why does the fit fail?**

<Choice v="a">The encoder is too noisy to measure $\ddot\theta$</Choice>
<Choice v="b">Only the sum $p_1 + p_2$ affects the data: the two regressor columns are (nearly) identical</Choice>
<Choice v="c">Damping hides the spring</Choice>
<Choice v="d">The sampling rate is too low</Choice>

<Solution>

With $\theta_m = 0$ the spring term is $-p_1\theta$, and for small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so the gravity term is $\approx -p_2\theta$. Only the sum $p_1 + p_2$ affects the data. The two regressor columns are (nearly) identical, the system matrix is (nearly) singular, and its null space is the direction $[1, -1]$: increasing $p_1$ while decreasing $p_2$ by the same amount changes nothing.

Fixes: (1) **drive the motor**, so $\theta_m - \theta$ is no longer proportional to $\theta$; (2) **swing to large angles**, where $\sin\theta$ differs from $\theta$. In self-modeling terms, *excitation determines what is identifiable*.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p6" title="Inverse dynamics" answer="0.29989" labels="θm (rad)" tol="0.001">

With $m=1$, $L=1$, $k=10$, $c=0.1$, $g=9.81$, you measure $\theta_a = 0.2$ rad, $\dot\theta_a = 0.5$ rad/s, $\ddot\theta_a = -1.0$ rad/s². What motor angle $\theta_m$ produced this?

*Enter $\theta_m$.*

<Solution>

From $I\ddot\theta = k(\theta_m - \theta) - mgL\sin\theta - c\dot\theta$:
$$\theta_m = \theta + \frac{I\ddot\theta + mgL\sin\theta + c\dot\theta}{k} = 0.2 + \frac{-1 + 9.81\cdot 0.19867 + 0.05}{10} = 0.2 + \frac{0.99895}{10} \approx 0.29989 \text{ rad}.$$

If your self-model's inverse-dynamics estimate of $\theta_m$ disagrees with the command you actually sent, something about the body (or the model) has changed. That residual is a simple fault detector.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p7" title="Why pivot?" choice="a">

Solve $\begin{bmatrix} 10^{-20} & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ by elimination *without* swapping rows, in 16-digit floating point. What goes wrong, and how does pivoting fix it?

**What does elimination without row swaps give for $x_1$?**

<Choice v="a">$x_1 = 0$: the important information was rounded away</Choice>
<Choice v="b">$x_1 = 1$: no problem at all</Choice>
<Choice v="c">$x_1 = 10^{20}$</Choice>
<Choice v="d">Division by zero, so the result is NaN</Choice>

<Solution>

Multiplier $= 10^{20}$. Row 2 becomes $[0,\; 1 - 10^{20}]$ with right side $2 - 10^{20}$. In floating point both round to $-10^{20}$, so $x_2 = 1$ (fine), but then $x_1 = (1 - x_2)/10^{-20} = 0$. The true answer is $x \approx [1, 1]$.
Swapping rows first makes the pivot $1$ and the multiplier $10^{-20}$, so nothing large is created and nothing important gets rounded away. Your build demo prints both versions.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Callout title="Self-model connection">

`inverseDynamics` is a complete, if crude, fault detector. Compare its output to the command you actually sent. On the nominal body the residual is near zero. If the true mass is $m + \Delta m$ but your model still assumes $m$, the residual works out to $-\Delta m\,L\,(L\ddot\theta_a + g\sin\theta_a)/k$. It oscillates, but it's *proportional to a signal you can compute*. Regress the residual on $L\ddot\theta_a + g\sin\theta_a$ and you recover the sign and size of $\Delta m$ at any drive frequency. An amplitude-based score can't do that. Keep it in mind for F3 (change detection).

</Callout>
