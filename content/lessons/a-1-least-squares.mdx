## Why this matters

You have far more measurements than unknowns: thousands of encoder samples, three physical parameters. No parameter set fits every sample exactly, so you pick the one that minimizes the squared error. That's least squares, and it's the core of system identification, of linear probes on world-model latents, and of the last layer of most regressors. For the compliant pendulum the dynamics are **linear in the parameters** even though they're nonlinear in the state, so a single solve gives you $k/I$, $g/L$ and $c/I$ from data. Bump the mass and refit on a sliding window: $k/I$ and $c/I$ drop by exactly $1/1.5$ while $g/L$ stays put. That's a body-change detector whose output is a *physical diagnosis*, not just a score.

## Core ideas

- **Overdetermined means more equations than unknowns.** $Ax = y$ with $A$ tall usually has no exact solution. Instead you minimize $\|Ax - y\|^2$.
- **The best fit is a projection.** $A\hat x$ is the point in the column space closest to $y$. The residual $r = y - A\hat x$ is perpendicular to every column: $A^\top r = 0$.
- **That gives the normal equations:** $A^\top A\,\hat x = A^\top y$, a small square system ($3\times 3$ here) that you solve with LU.
- **"Linear" means linear in the parameters.** $\ddot\theta = p_1(\theta_m - \theta) - p_2\sin\theta - p_3\dot\theta$ is linear in $p_1, p_2, p_3$. The $\sin$ just becomes a column of data.
- **Columns must be independent.** If two regressor columns are (nearly) parallel, $A^\top A$ is (nearly) singular and the split between those parameters is undetermined. Fix it with better *excitation*, not a better solver.
- **Residuals are a detector.** Freeze the fitted model and watch the prediction error. When the body changes, the residual jumps.
- **Sliding windows track change.** Refit on the last $N$ samples. Short windows react fast but are noisy; long windows are smooth but slow to react. It's the same trade-off as in CUSUM (F3).
- **Derivatives of noisy data are the real enemy.** Acceleration from a twice-differenced encoder is extremely noisy (Problem 6). Clean simulated data hides this.

## Problem set

<Problem id="p1" kind="compute" title="A three-point line fit">

Fit $y = a + bt$ to the points $(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)$ using the normal equations. Then check that the residuals sum to zero and are orthogonal to $t$.

<Solution>

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}$, $A^\top y = [5, 6]^\top$.
$\det = 6$, so $a = (5\cdot5 - 3\cdot 6)/6 = 7/6$ and $b = (3\cdot 6 - 3\cdot 5)/6 = 1/2$.
Residuals: $1 - 7/6 = -1/6$, $2 - 5/3 = 1/3$, $2 - 13/6 = -1/6$. Sum: $0$ ✓. Dot with $t = [0,1,2]$: $0 + 1/3 - 1/3 = 0$ ✓.

</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem id="p2" kind="concept" title="Why perpendicular?">

Explain in one picture-level paragraph why the best-fit residual must be perpendicular to every column of $A$.

<Solution>

$A\hat x$ can be any point in the column space, which is a flat subspace. The closest point of a flat subspace to $y$ is the foot of the perpendicular dropped from $y$. If the residual had any component *along* the subspace, you could slide $A\hat x$ in that direction and get closer. So at the optimum there's no component along any column: $A^\top r = 0$.

</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem id="p3" kind="derive" title="The pendulum as a regression">

Starting from $I\ddot\theta = k(\theta_m - \theta) - mgL\sin\theta - c\dot\theta$, write one row of the regression $y = Ax$ for one sample. What are $x$, the row of $A$, and $y$?

<Solution>

Divide by $I = mL^2$: $\ddot\theta = \frac{k}{I}(\theta_m - \theta) - \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta - \frac{c}{I}\dot\theta$.
$x = [k/I,\; g/L,\; c/I]$, row $= [\theta_m - \theta,\; -\sin\theta,\; -\dot\theta]$, $y = \ddot\theta$.
Note that $mgL/I = g/L$: mass cancels out of the gravity term.

</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem id="p4" kind="compute" title="Parameter signatures of different body changes">

Starting from $m = 1$, $L = 1$, $k = 10$, $c = 0.1$, give $[k/I, g/L, c/I]$ after (a) the mass is multiplied by 1.5, and (b) the length is doubled. Could a self-model tell these two faults apart?

<Solution>

Nominal: $[10, 9.81, 0.1]$.
(a) $I = 1.5$: $[6.667,\; 9.81,\; 0.0667]$. Only the $I$-scaled terms move.
(b) $I = 4$: $[2.5,\; 4.905,\; 0.025]$. All three move, and $g/L$ halves.
Yes: the *pattern* of parameter changes is a fingerprint of the fault. That's diagnosis, not just detection.

</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem id="p5" kind="concept" title="Excitation">

Your sliding-window fit is rock solid during an energetic multi-sine drive but swings wildly when the robot holds still. Why? What does $A^\top A$ look like when the robot is still?

<Solution>

When the robot holds still, $\theta$, $\dot\theta$ and $\theta_m - \theta$ are all near zero or constant. The columns of $A$ are tiny or proportional to each other, so $A^\top A$ is nearly singular and noise dominates the solve. Least squares can only learn about directions the data actually varies in. Practical fix: only update the self-model when the data is informative (watch the smallest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$), or add a prior (regularization).

</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem id="p6" kind="estimate" title="Differentiating an encoder twice">

Encoder noise is $\sigma = 10^{-3}$ rad (independent per sample) at $\Delta t = 0.002$ s. Estimate the noise standard deviation of $\ddot\theta \approx (\theta_{i+1} - 2\theta_i + \theta_{i-1})/\Delta t^2$. Compare it to the true acceleration amplitude $\approx 2.7$ rad/s² for a 0.3 rad swing at 3 rad/s.

<Solution>

Variance $= (1 + 4 + 1)\sigma^2/\Delta t^4$, so std $= \sqrt6\,\sigma/\Delta t^2 = 2.449\times10^{-3}/4\times10^{-6} \approx 612$ rad/s². The noise is more than **200× the signal**. Raw double differencing is useless here. You'd low-pass filter first, fit in the velocity domain, or integrate the model instead of differentiating the data. The clean demo below hides this, so keep it in mind.

</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem id="p7" kind="concept" title="Window length">

You refit every 0.5 s on a window of the last $W$ seconds. After the mass bump at $t_0$, roughly when does the estimate fully reflect the new mass? What do you trade by shrinking $W$?

<Solution>

The window is a mix of old and new data until it contains only post-bump samples, at $t_0 + W$. Before that, the estimate sits somewhere in between. Smaller $W$ means faster detection but fewer samples, so higher variance (and worse conditioning when excitation is weak). This detection-delay versus false-alarm trade-off is exactly what CUSUM and GLR (F3) formalize.

</Solution>
</Problem>
