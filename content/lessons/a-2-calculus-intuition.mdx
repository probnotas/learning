<Article id="why" title="Why this matters for your self-model">

Your pendulum's encoder reports one number, the angle. Your self-model, $I\ddot\theta_a = k(\theta_m - \theta_a) - mgL\sin\theta_a - c\dot\theta_a$, is written in **velocities and accelerations**. Everything between the encoder and the model is calculus: derivatives turn positions into rates, and the dynamics are a statement about second derivatives. Done carelessly, differentiation is also the biggest single source of noise in a learned forward model: a 4096-count encoder differentiated twice at 500 Hz gives accelerations that are ~100× noisier than the signal. This lesson builds intuition with 3Blue1Brown first, as the curriculum asks, because Feynman's oscillator chapters assume you're comfortable with $d/dt$, $d^2/dt^2$ and $e^x$.

</Article>

<Article id="ideas" title="Key ideas: derivatives as rates">

- **A derivative is a rate: "how much does the output move per unit of input, right here?"** $\dot\theta$ is radians per second; $\ddot\theta$ is radians per second, per second.
- **It's the limit of finite differences.** $\dot\theta(t) \approx \frac{\theta(t+h) - \theta(t)}{h}$, which gets better as $h \to 0$, until floating point and sensor noise bite.
- **Central differences are much better than forward ones.** Forward error shrinks like $h$; central error like $h^2$. Halve the step: forward error halves, central error quarters.
- **Derivative rules are geometry.** $\frac{d}{dt}\sin t = \cos t$; $\frac{d}{dt}t^2 = 2t$. The product rule and chain rule let you differentiate anything built from those.
- **Chain rule = rates multiply.** $\frac{d}{dt}\sin(\theta(t)) = \cos\theta\cdot\dot\theta$. The gravity torque changes at a rate set by both the geometry and how fast the arm moves.
- **$e^{at}$ is the function whose rate is proportional to itself.** That's why decay, growth, and (with $a$ complex) oscillation all look like exponentials.
- **Newton's law constrains the second derivative.** State = position and velocity; the dynamics tell you acceleration; integrate twice to move forward in time.
- **Differentiation amplifies noise.** Error from a sensor of resolution $\epsilon$ scales like $\epsilon/h$ for velocity and $\epsilon/h^2$ for acceleration. Smoothing reduces noise but adds **lag**.

</Article>

<Q id="p1" title="A sinusoid satisfies the oscillator equation" choice="c">

For $\theta(t) = 0.3\sin(3t)$, compute $\dot\theta$ and $\ddot\theta$. Express $\ddot\theta$ in terms of $\theta$. What equation does this sinusoid satisfy?

**Which relation holds?**

<Choice v="a">$\ddot\theta = -3\theta$</Choice>
<Choice v="b">$\ddot\theta = 9\theta$</Choice>
<Choice v="c">$\ddot\theta = -9\theta$</Choice>
<Choice v="d">$\ddot\theta = -0.9\theta$</Choice>

<Solution>

$\dot\theta = 0.9\cos 3t$, $\ddot\theta = -2.7\sin 3t = -9\,\theta$.
So $\ddot\theta + 9\theta = 0$: the harmonic oscillator equation with $\omega^2 = 9$. Next lesson runs this in reverse: start from $\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\theta$ and find that sinusoids are the solutions.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p2" title="Forward vs central" answer="0.852161;0.876121" labels="forward estimate;central estimate" tol="0.0001">

Estimate $\frac{d}{dt}\sin t$ at $t = 0.5$ with $h = 0.1$ using a forward difference and a central difference. Compare to $\cos 0.5 = 0.877583$.

*Enter both estimates.*

<Solution>

Forward: $(\sin 0.6 - \sin 0.5)/0.1 = (0.564642 - 0.479426)/0.1 = 0.852161$, error $\approx -0.0254$.
Central: $(\sin 0.6 - \sin 0.4)/0.2 = (0.564642 - 0.389418)/0.2 = 0.876121$, error $\approx -0.00146$.
Central is about 17× more accurate at the same $h$ and the same cost (two function values).

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p3" title="Chain rule on the gravity torque" answer="-18.7" labels="dτ/dt (N·m/s)" tol="0.01">

The gravity torque is $\tau_g = -mgL\sin\theta(t)$. Find $\dot\tau_g$. At $\theta = 0.3$ rad, $\dot\theta = 2$ rad/s, $m = L = 1$, how fast is the torque changing?

*Enter $\dot\tau_g$.*

<Solution>

$\dot\tau_g = -mgL\cos\theta\cdot\dot\theta = -9.81\cdot\cos(0.3)\cdot 2 = -9.81\cdot0.9553\cdot2 \approx -18.7$ N·m/s.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p4" title="Damping removes energy" choice="b">

With the motor held still at $\theta_m$, the compliant pendulum's energy is $E = \tfrac12 I\dot\theta^2 + mgL(1 - \cos\theta) + \tfrac12 k(\theta - \theta_m)^2$. Use the product and chain rules plus the equation of motion to show $\dot E = -c\,\dot\theta^2$.

**What do you get?**

<Choice v="a">$\dot E = 0$</Choice>
<Choice v="b">$\dot E = -c\,\dot\theta^2$</Choice>
<Choice v="c">$\dot E = -c\,\dot\theta$</Choice>
<Choice v="d">$\dot E = -k\theta\dot\theta$</Choice>

<Solution>

$\dot E = I\dot\theta\ddot\theta + mgL\sin\theta\,\dot\theta + k(\theta - \theta_m)\dot\theta = \dot\theta\left[I\ddot\theta + mgL\sin\theta + k(\theta - \theta_m)\right]$.
The equation of motion says the bracket equals $-c\dot\theta$, so $\dot E = -c\dot\theta^2 \le 0$. Energy only ever leaves, at a rate proportional to speed squared. When the motor *moves*, it does work on the spring, and that's how a drive pumps energy in at resonance.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p5" title="Exponential decay" answer="13.9;0.05" labels="half-life (s);fraction left after 60 s" tol="0.02">

The swing envelope of the lightly damped pendulum decays like $e^{-0.05t}$. What's its half-life? After 60 s, what fraction remains?

*Enter the half-life and the fraction remaining after 60 s.*

<Solution>

$e^{-0.05 t_{1/2}} = 1/2 \Rightarrow t_{1/2} = \ln 2/0.05 \approx 13.9$ s.
After 60 s: $e^{-3} \approx 0.05$, so 5% remains. "Wait for steady state" means a minute or more at these settings.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p6" title="What an encoder does to your derivatives" answer="0.38;383" labels="velocity error (rad/s);acceleration error (rad/s²)" tol="0.03">

A 4096-count/rev encoder has resolution $\epsilon = 2\pi/4096 \approx 1.53\times10^{-3}$ rad. At 500 Hz ($h = 0.002$ s), estimate the worst-case error from one count of quantization in (a) a central-difference velocity, (b) a second-difference acceleration. Compare to the true amplitudes for a $0.3\sin 3t$ swing.

*Enter both worst-case errors.*

<Solution>

(a) $\epsilon/(2h) \approx 0.38$ rad/s, versus a true peak velocity of 0.9 rad/s: about 40%.
(b) $\epsilon/h^2 \approx 383$ rad/s², versus a true peak acceleration of 2.7 rad/s²: roughly **140×** the signal.
Velocity is usable with smoothing; raw double-differenced acceleration is not. The build shows both.

</Solution>
</Q>

<Q id="p7" title="Smoothing costs time" answer="24" labels="lag (ms)" tol="0.05">

A trailing moving average over the last $N$ samples at sample period $h$ delays a slowly varying signal by about how much? Why does that matter for a self-model used for fault detection?

**For $N = 25$ and $h = 2$ ms, enter the lag in milliseconds.**

<Solution>

The average is centered on the middle of the window, so it lags by about $(N-1)h/2$. With $N = 25$ and $h = 2$ ms, that's 24 ms. For a 3 rad/s swing that's a phase lag of $3\times0.024 = 0.072$ rad, about 4°. A detector that compares a lagged velocity against an un-lagged model prediction sees a residual that has nothing to do with the body. Match the filtering on both sides, or use a zero-phase filter offline.

</Solution>
</Q>
